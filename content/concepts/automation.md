Automation is the replacement of human labor with machines. In market society this process creates a recurring tension: machines raise productivity and reduce the need for workers, yet the economy still depends on wage earners to buy the goods that machines produce.

Varoufakis traces this contradiction through history, from the Luddites to modern factories and offices. The book argues that automation is not new and does not automatically lead to mass leisure or shared prosperity. When machines displace workers, those workers lose the wages they need to consume. If enough people are displaced without new forms of income or redistribution, demand collapses and the system enters crisis. The profit motive drives firms to automate, but the same motive resists the redistribution that would keep consumption stable.

The author uses the image of a machine that can produce everything humans need but requires no workers. In such a world, the old link between work and income breaks. Market society has no built-in mechanism to handle this outcome because it assumes most people will continue to sell their labor. The book presents this as one of the central challenges that technology poses to the existing economic order — not a technical problem of building better machines, but a political problem of how to organize production and distribution once human labor is no longer required at scale.
