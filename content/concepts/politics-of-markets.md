The politics of markets is the recognition that markets are not natural or self-regulating but are shaped by rules that distribute power. These rules determine who can participate, on what terms, and who benefits from the outcomes.

Varoufakis argues throughout the book that the economy is a human creation whose rules can be changed. The separation that creates market society, the banking system that creates money, the labor market that sets wages — all rest on political decisions about property, credit, and contracts. The common claim that markets should be left alone or that economics is a technical subject separate from politics is itself a political position that protects the existing distribution of power.

The final chapter confronts the fantasy that technology or markets can solve problems that are fundamentally political. Even perfect machines or perfectly competitive markets would still require rules about ownership, access, and distribution. The book ends with the claim that genuine human flourishing requires the ability to shape those rules rather than treating them as given. The economy is always political; the only question is whether that politics is acknowledged and contested.
