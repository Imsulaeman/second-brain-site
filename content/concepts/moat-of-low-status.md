# Moat of Low Status

The moat of low status is the period you must cross when learning a new skill where you are visibly bad at it — asking obvious questions, making embarrassing mistakes, not knowing things others assume are common knowledge.

It is called a moat because:
1. You cannot leap to the other side. There is no shortcut past being bad before being good.
2. Anyone who can cross it gains a real advantage — most people can't tolerate the discomfort.

## The tradeoff

You can cross the moat quietly — by not asking questions, not collaborating, hiding your ignorance. But this significantly hampers learning ("it nerfs learning"). The "learn by doing" advice only works if you're willing to splash around visibly.

The implication: visible incompetence is not a side effect of learning; it is a necessary input to learning fast. People who treat it as a cost to be minimized are trading away the best feedback they could get.

## Thriving in the moat

Learning to thrive in the moat — rather than just tolerating it — is a qualitative threshold. Cate Hall's example: playing a hand badly in a major poker tournament, with cameras and reporters crowding around, then realizing she'd crossed some threshold of unflappability. The call was bad, but she made it for the right reason, and she looks back on it with fondness rather than pure embarrassment.

The tell that you've crossed this threshold: you can act under public scrutiny without that scrutiny distorting your decision-making.

## Relation to real edges

The moat is connected to why real edges exist at all. Most people don't pursue obvious high-value skills and actions because doing so requires crossing a moat — the discomfort of being seen as incompetent, low-status, or ridiculous during the learning phase. The moat is what keeps the edges available.
