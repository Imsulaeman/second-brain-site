# About-You Introduction

[[entities/Rebecca-Okamoto|Rebecca Okamoto]]'s distinction between introductions that describe *you* and introductions that describe what you can do for *them*.

Most people answer "Tell me about yourself" with credentials, titles, and accomplishment lists — an **about-me** introduction. The listener's question is rarely autobiographical. They want to know whether you can solve their problem.

## The Contrast

**About-me:** *"I'm an award-winning, best-selling author."*

**About-you:** *"I help new authors get published faster."*

One describes identity and status. The other describes a benefit to the listener. The difference is audible immediately.

## Why About-Me Fails

Okamoto learned this by rejection. She entered a supply-chain instructor interview with 20 years of P&G expertise and a pitch listing every accomplishment. The interviewer wanted to hear what she could do *for the institute*, not what she had done for herself. Smart, qualified, perfect fit — none of it mattered without capturing attention in the listener's frame.

The fix isn't hiding qualifications. It's leading with the listener's stake before (or instead of) your biography.

## Relation to Carnegie

This rhymes with [[concepts/talk-in-terms-of-interests|talking in terms of the other person's interests]] — enter their world first. Okamoto makes it operational for the opening sentence: the first 20 words should be about *their* benefit, not your pedigree.

A strong about-you intro also triggers [[concepts/let-them-talk|let them talk]] — the goal is "Tell me more," not a monologue.

## The Imposter Reframe

When rejections pile up, the inner critic often blames competence. Okamoto argues the failure mode may be structural: an about-me introduction makes qualified people sound self-focused. Before accepting the imposter narrative, test whether the introduction is oriented toward the listener.
