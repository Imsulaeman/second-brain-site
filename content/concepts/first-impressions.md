# First Impressions

The disproportionate weight of opening moments in how someone is evaluated, heard, and remembered. [[entities/Rebecca-Okamoto|Rebecca Okamoto]] treats the self-introduction as a first-impression lever: split-second attention spans mean you may have only seconds to turn "Tell me about yourself" into an opportunity.

## Why They Stick

First impressions set a frame that later information must overcome. [[concepts/halo-effect|Halo effect]] research shows interviewers who like a candidate in the first five minutes rarely recover when later answers are weak — System 1 seeks coherence and spreads early liking across the whole judgment.

Okamoto's supply-chain interview failure illustrates the inverse: a strong credential profile never got heard because the opening monologue was about her accomplishments, not the institute's needs. Rejected in five minutes on one answer.

## Sound Bite Logic

Okamoto studied first-impression science and viral headlines — what makes something irresistible enough to stop the scroll. The takeaway for introductions: design for attention capture, not completeness. A [[concepts/twenty-word-introduction|20-word sound bite]] that promises listener benefit outperforms a thorough autobiography when the goal is "Tell me more."

The attention-deficit framing is deliberately extreme (goldfish comparison), but the mechanism is real: overloaded listeners decide quickly whether to engage.

## Misattribution

When doors close repeatedly, people often blame intelligence or imposter feelings. Okamoto argues many closures are [[concepts/about-you-introduction|introduction failures]] — the person was qualified but framed wrong. Testing the opening sentence is cheaper than rewriting your competence story.
