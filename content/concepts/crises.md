Economic crises are periods when the normal circulation of goods, money, and labor breaks down on a large scale. In market society these events are not external shocks but emerge from the internal logic of debt, profit, and banking.

Varoufakis describes how the system depends on a continuous chain of promises about the future. When enough of those promises cannot be kept, lending stops, money creation reverses, and purchasing power disappears even though goods and workers are still available. The result is the destruction of usable wealth while people go without — factories idled, food destroyed, houses left empty. Both the Great Depression and the Greek debt crisis followed this pattern.

The book uses the metaphor of an ecosystem that must recycle oxygen and carbon dioxide. When the financial system stops recycling purchasing power, the economy does not simply slow down; it actively produces outcomes that make no sense from the perspective of human need. The same mechanism that drives expansion during good times generates these breakdowns when profit expectations collapse. The crises are therefore not failures of the system but expressions of how it actually works when the forward-looking claims that sustain it are called into question.
