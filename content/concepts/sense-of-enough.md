# Sense of Enough

One of [[entities/Howard-Stevenson]]'s three biggest surprises from 150 high-achiever interviews: the most satisfied people had a **reasoned sense of enough** across each dimension of their lives. The word "enough" barely appears in success literature — Samuel Gompers, asked what labor wanted, answered "more." But without enough, the maximization trap closes.

"Enough" has two bounds in English: a lower bound ("have you done enough?") and an upper bound ("that's enough!"). Both matter.

## Two Dimensions of Enough

**Dimensionality** — What's enough in each of the [[concepts/four-dimensions-of-success]]?
- What's enough achievement (in which specific sub-areas)?
- What's enough significance (for whom; doing what)?
- What's enough happiness (which activities actually produce it)?
- What's enough legacy (which contributions will I care about at the end)?

Without an answer to each, you default to more — and more in one dimension crowds out the others.

**Time frame** — What's enough for today, this week, this year, a lifetime?
- A daily enough enables completion: you can put work down with satisfaction, say "this was a good day," and come back tomorrow without guilt.
- A lifetime enough enables transition: once you've reached it, you can move on to the next stage rather than perpetually maximizing the current one.

## The Maximization Trap

There is no largest integer. You can always add one to anything. If you haven't defined enough, you'll never stop — and you'll crowd out dimensions you care about while doing it.

The emotional version: achievements feel meaningful when you define what you're reaching for. Without a ceiling, every milestone is just a new floor, and satisfaction never arrives.

## Why Enough Enables Juggling

[[concepts/success-as-juggling]] requires releasing balls. You can only throw a ball if you've decided you've done enough with it for now. Holding it means the others drop. Enough is the mechanism that makes the release possible.

## Benchmarks Stevenson Uses

- **Progress matters even with wealth.** Most people need to feel they're moving, not just holding. Accumulation without visible progress feels stagnant.
- **A personal balance sheet that includes giving.** Track what you've given away alongside what you've kept. This makes generosity measurable — and measurable means completable.
- **A daily task list that can actually be finished.** A list with 15 achievable items beats one impossible task. The ability to say "done" at day's end is a micro-enough — small but load-bearing for wellbeing.
- **Putting an activity down with satisfaction.** You can return to it tomorrow. The point is not that achievement stops; it's that today's portion is complete.

## The Setting-Limits Paradox

Counterintuitively: **setting limits increases the dimensionality of success.** By capping one dimension, you free attention and time for others. You're not shrinking your life — you're making room for the parts you've been crowding out.

Stevenson: "I'm sort of a shark. If I stop swimming, I die." Having enough doesn't mean stopping. It means knowing when to throw the ball.
