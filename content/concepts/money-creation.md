Money creation is the process by which new money enters circulation. In modern economies this happens primarily when banks make loans rather than when central banks print currency.

Varoufakis describes how a bank does not need existing deposits to issue a loan. When it approves credit, it simultaneously creates a deposit for the borrower. That new deposit is new money. The bank has expanded the money supply by the act of lending. This is the opposite of the common story that banks only lend out money that savers have already deposited. The book calls this "black magic" because the power is so consequential and so rarely stated plainly.

The same mechanism that creates money also creates debt. Every new unit of money comes into existence attached to a promise of repayment with interest. When the loans are repaid, that money disappears from circulation. When loans are not repaid, the money can remain but the banking system becomes fragile. The book uses this dynamic to explain both the expansion phases of economies and the sudden contractions that produce crises. The Greek debt crisis and the 2008 financial crisis both showed what happens when the chain of new lending breaks down.

Because banks create the money that makes the rest of the economy possible, decisions about who receives credit and for what purposes are decisions about what kind of economy will exist. The book insists this power is political, not technical, and that pretending otherwise has been a recurring source of instability.
