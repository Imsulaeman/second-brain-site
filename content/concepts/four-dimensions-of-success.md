# Four Dimensions of Success

[[entities/Howard-Stevenson]] and Laura Nash's research with ~150 high achievers identified four distinct types of success people report. The crucial finding: **the four are uncorrelated**. Getting one does not get you the others.

| Dimension | Whose judgment | Time frame | Core question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Achievement** | External (others striving for the same) | Present vs. a comparison class | What have I done against goals others also pursue? |
| **Significance** | Internal, outward-directed | Now → impact on others | Have I had a positive impact on the people I care about? |
| **Happiness** | Internal | Me/now | How do I feel about myself and my life? Am I content? |
| **Legacy** | External, future-directed | Long future | Have I done something to build upon? |

## Why They're Uncorrelated

Each dimension has its own emotional engine and its own failure mode:

- Marilyn Monroe and Ernest Hemingway: achievement without happiness
- Stevenson's grandfather (assistant postmaster, Silver Beaver Scout): significance without achievement
- Aspen trustafarians: happiness without achievement ("You told us to be happy, Dad. We're happy.")
- Karl Marx: enormous legacy while being mean, abusive, and chronically unhappy

Each dimension also has a **positive and a dark twin**:
- Achievement ← mastery, recognition, pride **or** envy, greed, fear
- Significance ← fairness, generosity, caring **or** power, self-aggrandizement
- Happiness ← contentment, fulfillment **or** laziness, gluttony
- Legacy ← altruism, generative desire **or** fear of death, need for control

Envy drives achievement just as well as mastery does — but it makes you miserable while doing so.

## The Three Great Fears

Most people operate under three implicit fears about this framework:

1. I won't be successful (on any dimension).
2. I will be successful — but it won't be enough (the Peggy Lee problem).
3. I will be successful — but I'll have to sell my soul to get there.

The third is the one most people carry longest: the belief that worldly success requires abandoning something essential.

## The Sequential Fallacy

The natural instinct: pursue dimensions one at a time. Achieve first, then find significance, then happiness, leave legacy at death. Several books sell this model (*From Success to Significance*, *Halftime*, etc.).

It doesn't work for three reasons:

1. **"When is enough to move on?" never arrives.** You can always add one to any number. Nobody wants to declare they've achieved enough.
2. **Emotional needs are continuous.** You can't exit the need for achievement or stop caring about the people you love. The need doesn't retire on schedule.
3. **Family won't wait.** "Your family will never wait. They find ways to cope if you don't deal with it now." The window for each dimension at each life stage closes.

## The Happiness Axis

Happiness is the dimension most resistant to deferral. "I will be happy when I have $100 million" is practically nonsense. Happiness is me/now — a present state, not a future reward. It is the only dimension where the goal is immediate: you either are happy now, or you are not. "Success is getting what you want; happiness is wanting what you get."

The practical test: know by your 30s what makes you happy, and seek it out on a regular basis. Don't postpone it.

## The Legacy Axis

Legacy is the dimension most resistant to self-definition. You don't define your own legacy — other people do. It is about your impact on the future. The attempts to control it (thousand-year trusts, naming rights) often reflect the dark twin: fear of death, not generative desire. Altruism is the prerequisite for legacy; holding onto control prevents building something that outlasts you.

Early legacy seeds matter more than late ones in Stevenson's research. The ripples from early career actions often turn out to be the most enduring.
