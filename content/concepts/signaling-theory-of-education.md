# Signaling Theory of Education

The value of a university degree comes primarily from what it signals to employers, not from the skills or knowledge it imparts.

The signal has three components:
1. **Intelligence** — getting through four years of coursework requires a baseline of cognitive ability
2. **Conscientiousness** — showing up, completing assignments, tolerating tedium
3. **Conformity** — doing what institutions ask, on their schedule, without disrupting the process

Employers value these traits. The degree certifies them. The specific content — the actual lectures, readings, and exams — is largely incidental to this certification function.

## Evidence

- Most of what's learned in school is forgotten within years; yet the degree retains its economic value
- Robin Hanson audited Stanford courses while working at NASA without paying tuition; a professor wrote him a recommendation letter. The free audit gave him the learning but not the signal
- Returns to education persist across fields unrelated to coursework content
- If learning were the goal, we'd expect online courses and self-study to be close substitutes for degrees. They largely aren't, for employment purposes
- Schools are structured in ways that actively interfere with learning (early wake times, high-stakes tests, memorization emphasis) but which effectively filter for compliance and endurance

## The waste diagnosis

Bryan Caplan (*The Case Against Education*): much educational spending is socially wasteful. Individual returns to education are real (you earn more), but they come largely by outcompeting others in credentialing, not by increasing your productive capacity. If everyone gets a degree, the signal inflates and the next rung (graduate school, elite institution) becomes necessary.

This does not mean education has no learning value — it does — but it means the private return and the social return diverge significantly.
