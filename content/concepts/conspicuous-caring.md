# Conspicuous Caring

Medicine and charity are partly about health and helping — and partly about publicly demonstrating that you care. The demonstration has value independent of the outcome.

The core analogy: when a toddler scrapes a knee, a mother's kiss produces no medical benefit, and yet both parties value the ritual. The toddler is reassured; the mother demonstrates loyalty and commitment. A similar dynamic runs through adult medicine at scale, just obscured by all the genuine healing that also happens.

## Evidence from medicine

- Randomized studies find that people given free healthcare consume significantly more medicine than control groups, yet don't end up noticeably healthier
- Non-medical interventions (stress reduction, diet, sleep, exercise) have larger effects on health outcomes but attract far less demand
- Patients show remarkably little interest in outcome data — only 8% of patients about to undergo dangerous heart surgery were willing to pay $50 to learn death rates at nearby hospitals
- Expensive end-of-life care is pursued even when cheap palliative care produces equivalent or better quality-of-life outcomes

If medicine were purely about health, these patterns wouldn't occur. They make sense if medicine is also about visibly demonstrating care — to the patient, to their family, and to the institution sponsoring their insurance.

## The caring coalition

The "mother" in adult medicine isn't just the doctor — it's everyone who helps along the way: the spouse who drives to the hospital, the employer who provides health insurance, the government that subsidizes it. Each party is demonstrating commitment and buying loyalty, not only pursuing health.

## Charity as conspicuous caring

The same dynamic applies to charitable giving. Singer's drowning child thought experiment reveals the gap: we'd immediately wade in to save a child drowning in front of us but don't donate equivalently to save statistically-equivalent distant children. The difference is the visibility of caring, not the child's need.

Warm-glow giving (James Andreoni): donors give partly for the internal reward of having given. The *act* of giving produces positive affect, independent of impact. This explains resistance to effective altruism — if you're giving for the warm glow, efficiency is orthogonal to your goal.
