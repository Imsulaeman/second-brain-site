**Deductive reasoning** starts with general rules, facts, or definitions and applies them to a specific case to reach a conclusion that must be true — provided the starting premises are correct and the logic is valid.

It does not deal in probability. It deals in necessity.

### How It Works in Practice

Classic structure:

- General rule: All humans are mortal.
- Specific fact: Socrates is human.
- Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

If the first two statements are true, the conclusion cannot be false. This is the power of deductive reasoning.

### Real-World Examples

**Law**  
Rule: A person under 17 cannot legally watch a certain movie without an adult.  
Fact: Dina is 15 and arrived without an adult.  
Conclusion: Dina cannot legally watch the movie.  
The conclusion follows necessarily from the rule and the fact.

**Company policy**  
Rule: Employees who work more than 40 hours in a week receive overtime pay.  
Fact: Maya worked 45 hours this week.  
Conclusion: Maya must receive overtime pay.  
The policy creates a binding conclusion once the condition is met.

**Technology**  
Rule: If a password is entered incorrectly five times, the account locks.  
Fact: The password was entered incorrectly five times.  
Conclusion: The account will lock.  
The system rule guarantees the outcome.

**Mathematics**  
When solving an equation, each algebraic step follows from established rules (distributive property, zero product property, etc.). If the initial equation is correct and every step obeys the rules, the final answer is necessarily correct. This is deductive reasoning in action.

**Everyday definition**  
Rule: All frozen water is ice.  
Fact: The water in the bottle is frozen.  
Conclusion: The water in the bottle is ice.  
The conclusion follows directly from the definition.

### Strengths and Weaknesses

Deductive reasoning gives certainty when the premises are true and the logic is valid. Its weakness is that it collapses if either the premise is false or the reasoning structure is invalid. A beautifully logical argument built on a false premise will still produce a false conclusion.

See also: [[Inductive Reasoning]] for the contrasting approach that moves from specific observations to probable general conclusions.
