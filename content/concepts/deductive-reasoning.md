**Definition**: Reasoning from known facts, rules, definitions, or principles to a specific conclusion that *must* be true if the premises are valid and the logic is sound.

**Core Structure**
- General rule or premise
- Specific fact or case
- Necessary conclusion

Classic example: All humans are mortal. Socrates is human. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

**Key Characteristics**
- Starts with rules/facts
- Produces certainty (when valid)
- Used in proofs, law, policy, mathematics, and formal arguments
- Fails only if premise is false or logic is invalid

**Strengths**
- Delivers logical necessity
- Excellent for applying established rules consistently

**Weaknesses**
- Completely dependent on the truth of starting premises
- Invalid structure produces false conclusions even with true premises

**Relation to Inductive Reasoning**
Deductive reasoning applies the general principles that inductive reasoning helps discover. See [[Inductive Reasoning]].
