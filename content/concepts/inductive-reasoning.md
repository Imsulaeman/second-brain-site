**Inductive reasoning** is the process of looking at specific observations and examples, noticing a pattern, and then forming a general conclusion that seems likely to be true.

It does not give certainty. It gives a **conjecture** — a reasonable guess based on the evidence you have so far.

### How It Works in Practice

Imagine you notice something repeating:

- The sun rose yesterday.
- The sun rose today.
- The sun has risen every single day you can remember.

From these specific observations, you form a general conclusion: “The sun will probably rise tomorrow.”

This is inductive reasoning. You moved from many specific cases to one broad prediction. The conclusion is useful and highly probable, but it is not guaranteed. One day the pattern could break (though in this case it almost certainly won’t).

### Real-World Examples

**Weather forecasting**  
A meteorologist observes that dark clouds, dropping air pressure, and high humidity have preceded rain many times before. Today those three conditions appear again. She concludes: “It will probably rain soon.” She is using inductive reasoning — turning past patterns into a current prediction.

**Medicine**  
A doctor sees ten patients with the same set of symptoms (fever, cough, fatigue) who all tested positive for the same virus. When an eleventh patient arrives with identical symptoms, the doctor thinks: “This patient probably has the same infection.” The conclusion is reasonable because it is built on repeated observations, yet the doctor still orders tests because similar symptoms can have different causes.

**Business**  
A shop owner notices that every time she runs a weekend discount, sales increase. After three successful weekends she concludes: “Weekend discounts probably increase sales.” She is generalizing from specific results. The pattern may continue, but other factors (holidays, weather, competitor actions) could change the outcome.

**Number patterns**  
Look at this sequence: 0, 3, 8, 15, 24, 35…  
Each number is one less than a perfect square (1²−1, 2²−1, 3²−1…). A reasonable conjecture is that the nth term follows the rule `n² − 1`. This inductive conclusion is useful for predicting the next numbers, but it remains a conjecture until proven.

### Strengths and Weaknesses

Inductive reasoning is powerful because it lets us learn from experience and make predictions when we lack complete information. Its weakness is that the pattern might not continue. The more examples you have, and the more carefully you control for other variables, the stronger the inductive conclusion becomes.
