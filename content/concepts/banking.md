Banking is the institution that creates most of the money in a modern economy and simultaneously moves it so that goods and services can be bought and sold. The two functions are inseparable. When banks lend, they create new deposits—new money—rather than simply transferring existing savings. When that lending stops or reverses, the money supply shrinks and the circulation of goods breaks down.

Varoufakis describes this as a form of black magic because the power is so rarely acknowledged. The public story treats banks as intermediaries between savers and borrowers. The actual process is closer to an ecosystem that must continuously recycle purchasing power. When the recycling fails, the result is not merely lower output but the active destruction of usable things—food destroyed, houses left empty, factories idled—while people who need those things go without. Both the 1930s and the Greek crisis demonstrated this outcome.

The political character of banking follows directly. Because banks create the money that makes the rest of the economy possible, decisions about who receives credit and on what terms are decisions about what the economy will produce and for whom. Treating these decisions as neutral technical matters has repeatedly produced instability. The book argues that the power to create and allocate money is always a political power, and pretending otherwise is what allows crises to recur.
