# Mental Models Latticework

Munger's central framework: acquire the big ideas from every major discipline — math, physics, biology, psychology, economics, engineering, history — and organize them into a latticework in your head that you use automatically for the rest of your life.

---

## Why a Latticework

Isolated facts are useless. Models must interconnect so that when a problem arrives, multiple models can be brought to bear simultaneously. The lattice structure means each new idea strengthens connections to existing ones, exactly like [[concepts/chunking]] in learning science.

Munger estimates ~100 models carry about 95% of the freight. You don't need to be an expert in every field — you need fluency in the big ideas.

---

## Key Models Munger Uses

| Discipline | Example Models |
|---|---|
| Math | Compound interest, permutations, decision trees, inversion |
| Physics | Critical mass, equilibrium, feedback loops |
| Biology | Evolution, adaptation, niche specialization |
| Psychology | All 25 tendencies in [[concepts/psychology-of-human-misjudgment]] |
| Economics | Comparative advantage, scale effects, incentives |
| Engineering | Redundancy, breakpoints, margin of safety |

---

## The Anti-Pattern: Man-with-a-Hammer

The opposite of the latticework is [[concepts/man-with-a-hammer-syndrome]] — knowing only one discipline and warping every problem to fit it. Munger's cautionary example is B.F. Skinner, who tried to explain all human behavior through incentives alone.

---

## Practice Requirements

Having the models is not enough. Like a concert pianist, you must practice using them continuously. This connects to [[concepts/deliberate-practice]] and the [[concepts/use-it-or-lose-it|Use-It-or-Lose-It]] tendency from Munger's psychology list. A checklist routine ensures no model is skipped when it matters.
