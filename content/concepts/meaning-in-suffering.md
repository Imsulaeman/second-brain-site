# Meaning in Suffering

The third and highest of logotherapy's three avenues to meaning: even unavoidable suffering carries within it the possibility of meaning, if the person chooses their attitude toward it.

This is not a consolation. It is a description of what Frankl observed repeatedly — in concentration camps, in clinical practice, in the lives of patients paralyzed, terminally ill, or bereaved — and confirmed empirically in Vietnam POW research.

## The Logic

When suffering is avoidable, the meaningful act is to remove its cause. Unnecessary suffering is masochistic, not heroic.

When suffering is *unavoidable* — when the situation cannot be changed and no exit exists — then the question shifts. The person can no longer ask "how do I escape this?" They can only ask "how do I meet this?" That question has an answer, and the quality of that answer is the avenue to meaning.

Frankl's formulation: suffering ceases to be suffering as soon as it acquires a meaning. Spinoza had anticipated this: "Emotion which is suffering ceases to be suffering as soon as we form a clear and precise picture of it."

## The Grieving Doctor

The most cited example from *Man's Search for Meaning*: an elderly doctor was deeply depressed after his wife died two years before. Frankl asked: "What would have happened if you had died first and your wife had had to survive you?" The doctor said: "Oh, for her this would have been terrible; how she would have suffered!" Frankl replied: "You see, such a suffering has been spared her, and it was you who have spared her this suffering; but now, you have to pay for it by surviving and mourning her."

The doctor said nothing, shook Frankl's hand, and left. His suffering had not decreased. But it had acquired a meaning: it was the price he paid to spare someone he loved a greater pain.

## Jerry Long

A later case that Frankl cited as the most powerful evidence for tragic optimism: Jerry Long was 17 when a diving accident left him a quadriplegic. In a letter to Frankl he wrote: "I view my life as being abundant with meaning and purpose. The attitude that I adopted on that fateful day has become my personal credo for life: I broke my neck, it didn't break me."

Long had not chosen to break his neck. He did decide not to let himself be broken by what had happened to him. The outer fact was fixed; the inner response was not.

## Having Been as the Surest Kind of Being

Frankl addressed the anxiety that the past is lost — that what has passed is gone. His counter: the past is the only thing that *cannot* be taken. The future is uncertain; the present passes. But what you have done, loved, and suffered is permanently inscribed. "In the past, nothing is irretrievably lost, but rather, on the contrary, everything is irrevocably stored and treasured."

This is especially relevant to meaning found in suffering. The patient who bore pain with dignity does not lose that achievement when the pain ends. It is lodged in the past, irrevocably.

## The Ape Analogy

Frankl used this to address the complaint that suffering is simply incomprehensible and therefore meaningless. An ape used in polio research cannot understand why it is being subjected to painful injections. The suffering appears senseless from inside the ape's frame. But the frame is too small to contain the meaning.

Similarly, what appears meaningless from within the human dimension may carry meaning in a wider frame that we cannot currently occupy. This is not a claim that meaning always exists; it is a caution against concluding it does not from within a narrow vantage.

## Tragic Triad

Frankl's broader frame — [[concepts/tragic-optimism|tragic optimism]] — deals with the three unavoidables: pain, guilt, and death. Meaning in suffering covers the pain vertex. The other two have their own translations:
- Guilt → opportunity to change oneself for the better
- Death → incentive to take responsible action while there is time
