Labor markets are the arena in which people sell their time and effort in exchange for wages. In market society this exchange is not optional for most people — it is the only way to access the necessities of life.

Varoufakis shows how labor markets differ from other markets because what is being sold is not a finished good but a person's capacity to work. The buyer (employer) cannot know in advance exactly what they are getting, and the seller (worker) cannot easily refuse the terms without risking survival. This asymmetry gives employers structural power in setting wages and conditions. The book uses historical and contemporary examples to show that wages are not simply determined by supply and demand in the abstract; they are shaped by the prior separation that forces people into the market in the first place.

The same logic explains why unemployment persists even when people are willing to work for very low wages. Employers do not hire every willing worker at the lowest possible price because doing so would undermine the discipline that unemployment exerts on the workforce. A reserve of unemployed people keeps wages from rising too quickly and maintains the threat that makes employed workers accept existing terms. The book connects this to both the Great Depression and more recent crises, where mass unemployment was not an accident but a feature of how labor markets function under the profit motive.

This view treats labor markets as political institutions rather than neutral mechanisms. The rules that govern hiring, firing, minimum wages, and union rights are decisions about power, not discoveries about efficiency. When those rules favor capital, the market clears at lower wages and higher unemployment than it would under different arrangements.
