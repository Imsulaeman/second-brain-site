Inequality in market societies is produced by the prior act of separating most people from direct access to the means of subsistence. It is not mainly the result of differences in talent, effort, or intelligence. Once people must sell their labor to live, those who own capital hold a structural position that lets them capture a disproportionate share of what is produced.

The book opens with this question and answers it by contrasting two situations. Aboriginal Australians lived for tens of thousands of years without the kind of inequality that appeared once market society arrived in Europe. The difference was not intelligence or culture but the historical process that turned land and tools into property that could be withheld. After that separation, profit and debt become the mechanisms that compound the initial advantage.

Varoufakis uses his own experience in Australia and Greece to show how this structure shapes daily life even for people who are not in immediate deprivation. The system produces both the wealth and the gaps. The usual claim that more growth will eventually close the gaps ignores the source of the inequality: the rules that force most people to sell their time while a smaller group owns the assets that generate profit from that labor.

Because the inequality is built into the separation that defines market society, it does not correct itself through expansion. It requires deliberate changes to the rules of access and ownership.
