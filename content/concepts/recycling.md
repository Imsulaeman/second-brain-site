Economic recycling refers to the continuous movement of purchasing power through the economy so that goods and services can be bought and sold. In market society this circulation depends on wages, credit, and the banking system working together.

Varoufakis compares the economy to a living system that must recycle oxygen and carbon dioxide. Money created through lending must reach people who will spend it, who in turn pay wages or buy from other producers, keeping the loop going. When banks stop lending or when debt burdens become too heavy, the recycling slows or reverses. Purchasing power is destroyed even though productive capacity remains.

The book shows that this recycling is not automatic. It requires the constant creation of new debt to sustain demand, and that debt in turn requires future profits to validate it. When the chain breaks, the result is not neutral stagnation but active waste: goods that could be used are left unused while people who need them have no money. The Greek crisis provided a clear recent example of how the breakdown of recycling produces outcomes that contradict any reasonable account of human welfare.
