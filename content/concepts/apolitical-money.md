The idea of apolitical money is the belief that money can function as a neutral medium of exchange without carrying power relations. The book argues this is a fantasy.

Varoufakis uses the example of a Second World War POW camp to demonstrate the point. Even in a highly controlled environment with no formal markets, prisoners quickly developed their own currencies, prices, and trading networks. Power relations emerged immediately around who controlled the medium of exchange and who could enforce contracts. The story shows that money is never just a technical tool; it is always embedded in relations of authority and dependence.

In market society this political character is even more pronounced because banks create most of the money through lending. Decisions about who receives credit, at what price, and for what purposes are decisions about what the economy will produce and who will benefit. Treating these decisions as neutral or purely technical obscures the power that is being exercised. The book insists that any attempt to design money as if it could be apolitical will either fail or simply hide the political choices under a technical veneer.
