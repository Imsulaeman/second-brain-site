There is only one reliable way to get another person to do anything: make them want to do it. Carnegie frames this as almost axiomatic — you can threaten someone into compliance, but then you've created an enemy. You can argue them into logical agreement, but they still may not act. The only method that produces genuine cooperation is finding what the other person already wants and showing them how your request connects to it.

Henry Ford put it plainly: "If there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from that person's angle as well as from your own." Carnegie calls this so obvious that 90 percent of people ignore it 90 percent of the time.

## The reframe before speaking

The technique is a question to ask yourself before any persuasion attempt: "How can I make this person want to do it?" Not "how do I convince them" — that framing keeps you thinking about your own logic. The Carnegie question forces you to model their situation, their interests, their current state of mind.

Drama critic William Winter observed that "self-expression is the dominant necessity of human nature." People don't just want things — they want to feel that the things they're doing express who they are. When you frame a request in terms that let the other person feel like an author rather than an object, you trigger that drive.

## Examples

**Carnegie's ballroom.** A New York hotel tripled his rent mid-season. Carnegie didn't complain. He went to the manager and quietly listed the disadvantages the hotel would face — ballroom tied up for lectures instead of profitable dances, loss of wealthy educated guests who would advertise the hotel simply by attending. The manager reduced the increase to 50 percent. Carnegie never said a word about what he wanted; he talked about what the manager wanted.

**Andrew Carnegie's nephews at Yale.** His sister-in-law's two boys were ignoring her letters. Carnegie bet he could get a reply by return mail without asking for it. He wrote a chatty letter, mentioned casually he was enclosing a five-dollar bill for each nephew — and didn't enclose it. Both nephews replied by return mail, thanking "Dear Uncle Andrew" warmly.

**Barbara Anderson's letter.** A bank employee wanting to relocate to Phoenix wrote to twelve banks, not about what she needed from them, but about what her ten years of banking experience could do for them and their growth. Eleven of twelve invited her to interview. She had a choice of offers. The one thing she didn't mention in the letter was what she wanted.

**Stan Novak and kindergarten.** His youngest son was screaming on the floor, refusing to go to kindergarten. Instead of forcing the issue, Novak sat down and thought: "If I were Tim, why would I be excited about kindergarten?" He and his wife made a list of everything Tim would get to do — finger painting, singing, new friends — and started doing those things themselves at the kitchen table. The next morning they found Tim asleep in the living room chair, waiting so he wouldn't be late.

**Carl and John.** Two insurance salespeople from the same company were assigned the same territory. Carl mentioned the new executive policy casually and moved on. John stopped two men on the sidewalk, told them excitedly they were among the first to be offered this policy, and had them signing applications before they even had all the details. John aroused an eager want. Carl just conveyed information.

## The ethics of it

Carnegie addresses the obvious objection: is this manipulation? His answer is that it isn't, as long as both parties benefit. In the ballroom negotiation, Carnegie got his rate and the hotel kept a steady customer. Barbara Anderson got a job; the bank got a skilled employee. The Shell station manager who was inspired to renovate saw his own sales improve. When both parties genuinely gain from the transaction, arousing an eager want isn't manipulation — it's alignment.

The manipulation version looks different: you manufacture a desire the other person doesn't have and wouldn't endorse on reflection, or you benefit at their expense. That's not what Carnegie describes.
