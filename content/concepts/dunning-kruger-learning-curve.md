# Dunning-Kruger Learning Curve

A four-stop map of how confidence and knowledge diverge and re-converge while learning a new skill. The underlying psychological finding — that people with low competence in a domain tend to overrate their own ability, because the same skill needed to perform well is the skill needed to judge performance accurately — is David Dunning and Justin Kruger's. [[entities/Vinh-Giang|Vinh Giang]] takes their curve and relabels it as a specific journey with named destinations, applied to communication skill.

## The Four Stops

1. **The start** — low knowledge, low confidence. Where everyone begins in a new domain.
2. **Peak of Mount Stupid** — knowledge has barely increased, but confidence has spiked. A person who's watched two podcasts and five YouTube videos on a topic starts talking like an expert, until a real question exposes the gap.
3. **Valley of Despair** — knowledge keeps climbing, but confidence collapses. This is the counterintuitive part: more knowledge, less confidence, because "the more you know, the more you realize you don't know," and because the material starts to feel overwhelming ("drinking from a fire hose").
4. **Slope of Mastery** (Dunning and Kruger's own term is the "slope of enlightenment") — the climb out, available to anyone regardless of which of the first three stops they're currently stuck at.

## Why the Valley Deepens Instead of Resolving

Giang's specific addition to the standard curve is a mechanism for why people don't naturally climb out of the Valley of Despair on their own: algorithm-driven content consumption. Learning in 15-second, randomly-sequenced fragments — the default mode of scrolling social media — produces the *feeling* of learning (dopamine hits, "save for later") without ever converting into applied skill, because nothing gets structured or revisited. He names this failure loop the [[concepts/cycle-of-doom|cycle of doom]]: get triggered to learn something, search, get overwhelmed by millions of results, give up and consume unrelated content instead, get triggered again months later, repeat. The valley gets deeper with each pass, not shallower, because knowledge is accumulating without ever being applied.

The way out, in his framing, is the [[concepts/mastery-cycle|Mastery Cycle]] — the discipline of applying what's learned immediately rather than continuing to stockpile more input.

## Distinguishing From the Plateau of Latent Potential

This curve is easy to conflate with James Clear's [[concepts/plateau-of-latent-potential|Plateau of Latent Potential]] ("Valley of Disappointment"), but the two describe different axes of the same general problem — effort not immediately paying off. Clear's plateau is about *compounding delay*: a sound method that is quietly working, with results simply not visible yet, like an ice cube not melting until the temperature crosses a threshold. The risk there is quitting a method that would have worked. Dunning-Kruger's valley is about *miscalibrated confidence*: knowledge and self-assessment moving in opposite directions as competence genuinely grows. The risk there is not quitting too early on a good method — it's mistaking a normal, temporary confidence crash for evidence that you're failing, when it's actually evidence that your self-assessment is finally becoming more accurate.
