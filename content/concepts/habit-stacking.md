# Habit Stacking

Habit stacking links a new behavior to an existing one using the formula:

> "After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]."

It is a special form of [[concepts/implementation-intentions|implementation intention]] — instead of specifying time and location, you use a behavior you already perform as the cue. Developed by BJ Fogg as part of his Tiny Habits program.

---

## Why It Works

Existing habits are already encoded as strong neural pathways. Stacking attaches a new behavior to an established cue rather than inventing one from scratch. The current habit fires automatically; the new habit rides that momentum.

The mechanism is a positive version of the [[concepts/diderot-effect|Diderot Effect]]: one behavior leads naturally into the next, creating a chain.

---

## Building Chains

Stacks can extend into full sequences — a morning or evening routine is often a long habit stack in disguise:

**Morning:**
- After I pour my morning coffee → meditate for 60 seconds
- After I meditate → write my to-do list for the day
- After I write my to-do list → begin my first task immediately

**Evening:**
- After I finish dinner → put my plate in the dishwasher
- After I put dishes away → wipe down the counter
- After I wipe the counter → set out my coffee mug for tomorrow

New behaviors can also be inserted into the middle of existing routines. If you want to read more each night: after making your bed, place a book on your pillow. Now the book is waiting when you climb in.

---

## Rules for Effective Stacking

**1. Choose the right anchor.** The existing habit must happen at the right time and frequency. If you want a daily habit but stack it on something that only happens on Mondays, the stack breaks.

**2. Be specific.** "After I close my laptop for lunch" beats "sometime in the morning." Ambiguity kills stacks. Clear's own push-up habit failed when his trigger was "when I take a break for lunch" — fixed once he changed to "when I close my laptop for lunch, I will do ten push-ups next to my desk."

**3. Match context.** The anchor must happen in the right setting. Stacking meditation onto a chaotic morning with kids in the room will fail — not because the intention is wrong, but because the context fights it.

**4. Start small.** Pair with the [[concepts/two-minute-rule|Two-Minute Rule]] to keep the new habit easy enough that it doesn't break the chain.

---

## General Habit Stacks

Stacks can also govern behavior in recurring situations rather than timed sequences:

- When I see a set of stairs → take them instead of the elevator
- When I walk into a party → introduce myself to someone I don't know
- When I want to buy something over $100 → wait 24 hours before purchasing
- When I serve myself a meal → always put vegetables on my plate first
- When I buy a new item → give something away ("one in, one out")
