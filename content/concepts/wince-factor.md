# Wince Factor

[[entities/Howard-Stevenson]]'s term for the involuntary emotional sting of seeing someone else succeed where you chose not to compete.

His example: he was the "ST" in Baupost Group, which became one of the most successful hedge funds in the world. He left when he became a single parent. When he opens Forbes and sees the person who took his place on the list — he winces. "Yeah, I wince. But then I think, if I had stayed in that path, I couldn't have done the other things I've done. But I still wince. I have to admit that."

## Wince ≠ Regret

The wince factor is a precise emotional distinction:

- A **wince** is an involuntary response to counterfactual success — the path you could have been on, had you chosen differently.
- **Regret** is a verdict: I made the wrong decision.

You can wince without regret. The wince is the honest acknowledgment that a path not taken had real value; the absence of regret is the honest acknowledgment that the chosen path was worth it. Both can be true simultaneously.

Suppressing the wince is dishonest. Letting the wince become regret is confusion — conflating the *price* of a choice with the *wrongness* of the choice.

## The Healthy Response

Stevenson's formulation: "I don't feel I made the wrong decisions. I just wince and say, that was a choice I chose. I chose it consciously, and I have no regrets."

The sequence:
1. Notice the wince. Don't suppress it — that's suppressing truth.
2. Trace what you got by choosing the other path. What became possible that wouldn't have been?
3. Hold both: the path not taken had value; the path taken also had value; the choice was made honestly.
4. The wince stays; the regret doesn't form.

## The Connection to Regret as Process

Stevenson's rule on regret: regrets come from kidding yourself — not from bad outcomes. If you acted honestly on the best available information, things going wrong gives you nothing to regret, because the process was sound. The wince acknowledges the cost of a choice that was made honestly. Regret would imply you cheated yourself.

This connects to [[concepts/decision-quality-vs-outcome]]: judging the decision by the process and the information available at the time, not by the counterfactual path you can see in hindsight.
