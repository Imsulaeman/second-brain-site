# Costly Signaling

A signal is reliable only if it's costly to fake. Cheap talk can be produced by anyone regardless of quality; a costly signal can only be produced (sustainably) by those who genuinely have the underlying trait being signaled.

The Arabian babbler example: these birds *compete to give* food and assistance to others, even at personal cost. The behavior looks altruistic but is actually a dominance display — advertising "I'm so fit that I can afford to be generous." A weak bird can't sustain the cost.

Peacock tails are the canonical example: an elaborate, metabolically expensive tail signals genetic health. Only healthy peacocks can afford one. The waste is the point.

## Costly signals in human behavior

- **Art** — time-intensive, cognitively demanding creative work signals surplus cognitive capacity (Geoffrey Miller's fitness-display theory)
- **Education** — years of costly credentialing signals intelligence and conscientiousness to employers (see [[concepts/signaling-theory-of-education]])
- **Religious observance** — costly rituals (Hajj, fasting, tithing) signal sincere group commitment; free-riders can't easily fake them
- **Charitable giving** — visible generosity signals wealth and virtue (see [[concepts/competitive-altruism]])
- **Conspicuous consumption** — expensive purchases signal wealth and taste (see [[concepts/conspicuous-consumption]])

## The key insight for institutions

Many institutions we think of as optimizing for stated goals (health, learning, helping others) are also — and sometimes primarily — mechanisms for expensive public commitments. The cost isn't a bug; it's what makes the signal legible.

This is why improving efficiency often fails to improve institutions: if you make the signal cheaper, it becomes less reliable, which undermines its social function even if it achieves the stated purpose better.
