# Learning from Failure

## Core Idea

Learning from failure sounds automatic, but it is not. A mistake only teaches when the learner can both emotionally tolerate the setback and accurately diagnose what actually went wrong.

## How It Works

Failure often hurts self-image. People like to experience themselves as capable, and a serious mistake can feel less like information and more like identity damage. If the blow is strong enough, attention shifts from learning to self-protection.

Even when the learner stays emotionally open, failure may still be hard to use because it is ambiguous. A bad outcome can have many causes: lack of effort, wrong strategy, poor timing, bad luck, unfair testing conditions, or misunderstanding of the task itself. Success often gives a cleaner pattern to copy than failure gives a pattern to repair.

## Example

The rune-language study gives a strong laboratory version of this problem. Participants who were cast as early failures continued to underperform later even though everyone entered the second round with the same information. The failure itself changed the psychological context of learning.

The French-class survey adds another layer: beginners often prefer encouragement, while advanced students are more open to direct correction. Tolerance for failure is partly developmental. It depends on investment, competence, and whether the learner already has enough stability to metabolize criticism.

## Why It Matters

This concept adds caution to the growth-oriented part of the wiki. [[concepts/growth-mindset|Growth mindset]] and resilience matter, but they do not make all failure equally useful. It also connects to [[concepts/deliberate-practice|deliberate practice]], where feedback is only valuable if the learner can process it, and to [[concepts/problem-framing|problem framing]], because vague failure often reflects vague diagnosis.
