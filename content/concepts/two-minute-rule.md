# Two-Minute Rule

When starting a new habit, scale it down to something that takes two minutes or less.

> "When you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do."

The goal is not to do something small forever. The goal is to master the art of showing up — because **a habit must be established before it can be improved.**

---

## The Twyla Tharp Example

Tharp is widely regarded as one of the greatest choreographers of the modern era. She credits much of her success to a simple daily ritual: "I wake up at 5:30 AM, put on my workout clothes, my leg warmers, my sweat shirt, and my hat. I walk outside my Manhattan home, hail a taxi, and tell the driver to take me to the Pumping Iron gym."

She notes: *"The ritual is not the stretching and weight training I put my body through each morning at the gym; the ritual is the cab. The moment I tell the driver where to go I have completed the ritual."*

Hailing the cab is the two-minute version of a two-hour workout. Once the cab is hailed, the rest follows automatically.

---

## Gateway Habits

The two-minute version is a *gateway habit* — the minimum action that leads naturally into the larger behavior:

| Full Habit | Two-Minute Version |
|---|---|
| Read before bed | Read one page |
| Do 30 minutes of yoga | Take out the yoga mat |
| Study for class | Open my notes |
| Run three miles | Tie my running shoes |
| Fold the laundry | Fold one pair of socks |

The first action is the decisive one. Once begun, continuing is far easier than not starting at all. 40–50% of daily actions are habitual — and habits shape the conscious choices that follow them. Tharp's cab doesn't just get her to the gym; it forecloses the alternative.

---

## Standardize Before You Optimize

The failure mode: trying to engineer a perfect habit before the basic one exists. Clear's principle: **standardize before you optimize.** You can't improve a habit that doesn't exist.

This is why the two-minute version is not just a trick to get started — it's the right strategy. The more you ritualize the beginning of a process, the more likely you can slip into deep focus. A consistent warm-up before every workout makes peak performance easier to enter. A creative ritual makes the hard work of creating easier to start.

---

## Strict Enforcement

For people who see through the strategy ("I know I'm just supposed to do more"), Clear suggests making the two-minute version the whole habit: *go for a run, but you must stop after two minutes. Start meditating, but you must stop after two minutes.*

One reader used this to lose 100 pounds. He went to the gym every day but told himself he wasn't allowed to stay more than five minutes. After a few weeks, he was already there anyway and started staying longer. The weight was gone within a few years.

Greg McKeown built a daily journaling habit by always stopping before it seemed like work — writing less than he felt like, every time. Hemingway's version: "The best way is to always stop when you are going good."

---

## Identity Connection

The two-minute rule also builds identity. Five consecutive days at the gym — even for two minutes — casts votes for the identity "person who doesn't miss workouts." The point isn't performance; it's proof. Each two-minute session is evidence you are becoming the type of person you want to be.

This connects directly to [[concepts/identity-based-habits|identity-based habits]]: the ritual is the vote.
