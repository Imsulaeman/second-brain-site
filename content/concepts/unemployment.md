Unemployment is the condition in which people who are willing and able to work cannot find buyers for their labor. In market society this is not an accident or temporary malfunction but a structural feature that serves a purpose.

The book explains that a pool of unemployed workers disciplines those who still have jobs. When unemployment is high, employed workers accept lower wages and harsher conditions because the alternative is joining the reserve army of the unemployed. Employers benefit from this pressure because it keeps labor costs down and maintains the threat that makes the existing workforce compliant. The result is that labor markets rarely clear at full employment; instead they stabilize at a level of unemployment that protects profit margins.

Varoufakis connects this mechanism to historical episodes such as the Great Depression and the Greek crisis. In both cases, mass unemployment was allowed to persist because reducing it would have required policies that threatened the existing distribution of power between capital and labor. The book treats unemployment not as a failure of the market to match supply and demand, but as evidence that the market is working as designed under the rules of market society.

This view makes unemployment a political outcome rather than a technical one. Changing the level of unemployment requires changing the balance of power that produces it — through policy, organization, or shifts in the underlying rules of access to resources.
