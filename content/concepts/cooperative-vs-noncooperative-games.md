# Cooperative vs. Non-Cooperative Games

Game theory divides interactions into two fundamental types based on whether players share goals and can make binding agreements.

## Cooperative Games

Players on the same side: shared goals, freely exchanged resources and information, mutual benefit actively pursued. Examples: teammates in a sport, business partners, roommates (in theory), international alliances.

Strategy in cooperative games is about coordination — how to divide effort, share information, and allocate gains fairly. Conflict exists but it's internal (how to split the pie) rather than external (whether to fight over it).

## Non-Cooperative Games

Players act independently in their own interest, potentially at the expense of others. There are winners and losers. Examples: competing businesses, political rivals, arms races, split-or-steal game shows.

Non-cooperative games are far more prevalent in the world and generate the more interesting strategic problems — because each player must model what others will do without knowing their choices in advance.

The [[concepts/prisoners-dilemma]] is a non-cooperative game. So is most of market competition, negotiation, and geopolitics.

## The Important Subtlety

The same people can be in both types simultaneously. Teammates cooperate internally but compete non-cooperatively against other teams. Business partners cooperate on their shared venture but compete with each other's personal interests. Real situations layer both structures.

And non-cooperative games can produce cooperative outcomes — through [[concepts/iterated-games]], reputation, and strategies like [[concepts/tit-for-tat]]. The game type describes the structure, not the inevitable outcome.
