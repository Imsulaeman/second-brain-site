# Weak Ties

Weak ties are low-intensity social connections — brief interactions with acquaintances, neighbors, shop workers, baristas, and strangers — as opposed to strong ties with close friends and family.

Mark Granovetter's original research showed weak ties outperform strong ties for information and opportunity. A close friend knows the same things you know and moves in the same circles. A casual acquaintance connects you to different worlds.

The health research extends this further: regular small interactions with people outside your close circle reduce isolation, provide low-stakes social practice, and create a felt sense of belonging to a wider community. The health benefit is measurable even when the interactions are brief and with people you'll never see again.

## Why This Matters for Loneliness

Most thinking about loneliness focuses on close relationships. The weak ties research shows this is too narrow. A person with no close friends but many warm daily interactions with neighbors, shop owners, and regulars may be less lonely — and healthier — than someone with strong friendships but no incidental daily contact.

The practical implication: the cure for [[concepts/loneliness]] doesn't require finding a new best friend. It can start with a stranger's comment on your hat. Small, real connections accumulate.
