# Can You Outsmart the False Cause Fallacy?

**Author:** [[entities/elizabeth-cox|Elizabeth Cox]]
**Type:** Educational reasoning essay

---

## Core Premise

The piece uses the history of childbed fever to show how people confuse correlation with causation. The key mistake is assuming that because two things appear together, one must be causing the other.

---

## Historical Example: Childbed Fever

Charles Meigs believed abdominal inflammation caused childbed fever because inflammation was commonly observed in women who died from it. The essay shows why this was weak reasoning:

- the inflammation may have been an effect, not a cause
- both symptoms may have come from a third factor
- observation alone was not enough to establish the true mechanism

The better explanation emerged when Oliver Wendell Holmes and [[entities/ignaz-semmelweis|Ignaz Semmelweis]] noticed transmission patterns involving physicians, autopsies, and contaminated hands. Later, [[entities/louis-pasteur|Louis Pasteur]] supplied the germ-based explanation.

---

## Main Lesson

The point is not merely historical. The essay argues that careful reasoning requires:

- skepticism toward surface associations
- attention to alternative explanations
- testing hypotheses through intervention
- willingness to revise accepted views when evidence improves
