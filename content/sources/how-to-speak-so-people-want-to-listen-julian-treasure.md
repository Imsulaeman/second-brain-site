# How to Speak So That People Want to Listen

[[entities/Julian-Treasure|Julian Treasure]]'s TED talk on why people stop listening and what speakers can change — both in *what* they say and *how* they say it. The argument moves from habits that repel listeners, through a four-part ethical foundation for speech, into the vocal toolbox most people never open, and closes with a vision of conscious sound in a noisy world.

## The Problem

The human voice is the instrument everyone plays, and the only sound that can start a war or say "I love you." Yet many people speak and are not heard. Treasure's answer: certain speaking habits push listeners away before content gets a chance.

## Seven Deadly Sins of Speaking

Treasure names seven habits to avoid — not an exhaustive list, but large enough to matter:

1. **Gossip** — speaking ill of someone absent; the gossiper will gossip about you five minutes later
2. **Judging** — hard to listen when you know you're being judged and found wanting
3. **Negativity** — chronic pessimism makes speech exhausting to receive
4. **Complaining** — "viral misery" that spreads darkness instead of lightness
5. **Excuses** — the "blamethrower" who passes responsibility instead of owning action
6. **Embroidery / exaggeration** — inflation of language that eventually becomes lying
7. **Dogmatism** — conflating opinions with facts; bombarding others with beliefs stated as truth

Full treatment: [[concepts/seven-deadly-sins-of-speaking]].

## HAIL: What to Stand On

The positive counterweight is an acronym — **HAIL** — meaning to greet or acclaim enthusiastically:

- **H**onesty — true, straight, clear
- **A**uthenticity — being yourself; "standing in your own truth"
- **I**ntegrity — being your word; doing what you say
- **L**ove — wishing people well (not romantic love); tempers brutal honesty and makes simultaneous judgment nearly impossible

Full treatment: [[concepts/hail-framework]].

## The Vocal Toolbox

What you say matters, but so does how you say it. Treasure treats the voice as an underused toolbox:

| Tool | What it does |
|------|-------------|
| **Register** | Where the voice sits — nose, throat, or chest. Chest register carries weight; we vote for politicians with lower voices because depth signals authority |
| **Timbre** | How the voice *feels* — rich, smooth, warm. Trainable through breathing, posture, and coaching |
| **Prosody** | The sing-song meta-language that imparts meaning; monotone speech is hard to listen to; uptalk (every sentence ending like a question) restricts communicative range |
| **Pace** | Speed for excitement or slowness for emphasis; silence is a tool, not a failure |
| **Pitch** | Shifts meaning ("Where did you leave my keys?" vs. the same words at higher pitch) |
| **Volume** | Loud for excitement, quiet to command attention |

**Sodcasting** — broadcasting your sound carelessly onto people around you — is the volume failure mode to avoid.

Full treatments: [[concepts/vocal-delivery-toolbox]], [[concepts/prosody]], [[concepts/sodcasting]].

## Vocal Warmup

Before any important speech — stage talk, marriage proposal, raise request — warm the engine. Treasure demonstrates six exercises:

1. Arms up, deep breath in, sigh out (ahhhh) — twice
2. Lip warmup: Ba ba ba ba ba ba ba ba
3. Lip trill: brrrrrrrrrr
4. Tongue: exaggerated la la la la la la la la la
5. Rolled R: rrrrrrr
6. **The siren** — we (high) to aw (low): weeeaawww, weeeaawww

Full treatment: [[concepts/vocal-warmup]].

## The Larger Vision

Treasure closes with a contrast: we currently speak poorly to people who aren't listening in environments full of noise and bad acoustics. The alternative is speaking powerfully to people listening consciously in spaces designed for sound — creating and consuming sound consciously until understanding becomes the norm.
