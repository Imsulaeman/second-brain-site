# Fortune's Formula

**Author:** [[entities/William-Poundstone|William Poundstone]]
**Type:** Book (Hill and Wang, 2005) — narrative history of the Kelly criterion
**Raw:** `raw/Fortune&_039_s Formula_ ... libgen.li (ingested).epub`
**Title origin:** Thorp's January 1961 AMS talk — *"Fortune's Formula: A Winning Strategy for Blackjack"* — which Poundstone borrowed with Thorp's approval.

---

Poundstone connects information theory, gambling, and Wall Street through one idea: **size your bets to maximize long-run geometric growth**, never bet so much that variance wipes you out.

## Part I — Entropy: Shannon, Thorp, Kelly

[[entities/Claude-Shannon|Claude Shannon]] invented information theory at Bell Labs. Shannon's insight: communication, randomness, and gambling share a math of **entropy and optimal encoding**.

[[entities/Edward-Thorp|Ed Thorp]] — mathematician, late-talker child prodigy — built the first rigorous blackjack card-counting system. Shannon funded Thorp's roulette experiments ($1,500 reconditioned wheel), co-authored the wearable computer paper, and in **November 1960** handed Thorp Kelly's 1956 paper.

[[entities/John-Kelly-Jr|John L. Kelly Jr.]] (Bell Labs, 1956) framed the problem as a **gambler with a private wire**: noisy tips on horse races. Greedy = bet entire bankroll on a tip → eventual wipeout when a tip fails. Timid = minimum bet → can't capitalize. Kelly: bet a **fraction** proportional to edge.

Kelly's broader form: **investment differs from gambling only by a minus sign.** Favorable bets = investments; unfavorable = gambling.

## Part II — Blackjack

Thorp's 1961 "Fortune's Formula" talk hit the AP wire — gamblers nationwide called for his system. The Kelly criterion, under the hood:

- With **no edge**, Kelly says don't bet (unlike martingale fantasies).
- With edge, proportional betting **prevents gambler's ruin** — you never stake 100% of current bankroll.
- Thorp became the first documented **sure winner** against Nevada blackjack (with Emmanuel Kimmel-backed bankroll).

## Part III — Arbitrage on Wall Street

Thorp's **Princeton Newport Partners** (1969–1980s): convertibles, warrants, statistical arbitrage — Kelly-style sizing in securities. Beat the market for years until Newport office raid (1987) unrelated to strategy quality.

Parallel cast: Paul Samuelson (skeptic of beat-the-market), Michael Milken, Robert Merton — the quant revolution Poundstone tracks toward LTCM.

## Part IV — St. Petersburg & Geometric Mean

Daniel Bernoulli (1738): evaluate risky ventures by **geometric mean** of outcomes, not arithmetic mean — presaging Kelly/log utility. The St. Petersburg paradox motivated logarithmic utility centuries before Kelly formalized bet sizing.

**Shannon's Demon:** a rebalancing scheme on volatile assets that extracts growth from noise alone — geometric-mean thinking in disguise.

Academic feud: Henry Latané's geometric-mean portfolio work vs. Markowitz mean-variance orthodoxy; Samuelson's one-syllable-word polemic (*"Why We Should Not Make Mean Log of Wealth Big Though Years to Act Are Long"*) attacking Kelly fans.

## Part V — LTCM and Overbetting

**Long-Term Capital Management** (Meriwether, Merton, Scholes): spectacular returns, then 1998 collapse. Press blamed leverage, fat tails, hubris. Poundstone's sharper word: **overbetting** — a gambling concept, not standard econ theory.

LTCM's effective leverage on some positions approached infinity (no-money-down structures). Two Nobel laureates crawling out of the wreckage made Kelly sizing impossible to ignore in academia afterward.

**Survival motive:** portfolio models are tools; humans choose policy. Overbetting is always bad.
