# Art of Trading - with Light, Su Zhu, and Hasu

This long-form Uncommon Core conversation brings together [[entities/light-crypto|Light]], [[entities/su-zhu|Su Zhu]], and [[entities/hasu|Hasu]] for two overlapping discussions: first, what good trading process in crypto actually looks like; second, why Bitcoin may be structurally advantaged over altcoins as crypto's monetary shelling point. The most durable parts of the interview are not the dated market calls. They are the operating principles underneath them.

The first part of the conversation treats trading as a changing zero-sum game closer to poker than to ordinary investing. Light's background in equities, options, poker, and then crypto leads him to a view of markets as adaptive rule systems where edges decay and where survival depends on asymmetry, pattern recognition, and process discipline rather than on being "right" in some ego sense. He argues that crypto is still soft enough to reward capable generalists, because there are many separate games being played at once and many participants are still naive. That is why he blends behavioral reading, asymmetric information, and old-fashioned [[concepts/tape-reading-and-order-flow|tape reading and order flow]] instead of committing to one narrow style.

From there the interview gets much more practical. Both Light and Su Zhu keep returning to the same point: a trade needs a thesis, an invalidation, and a payoff structure worth taking. If there is no clear invalidation, there is no real risk management. If the downside and upside are not shaped well, the game becomes structurally dangerous even before psychology enters. This section maps closely onto the wiki's existing cluster around [[concepts/trading-edge]], [[concepts/position-sizing]], and [[concepts/decision-quality-vs-outcome]]. They frame the beginner's biggest leak not as lack of intelligence but as refusal to be wrong early, which then leads to oversizing, thesis drift, averaging down without structure, and eventual ruin.

Another important strand is how they think about market learning itself. Light emphasizes process over outcome in almost exactly the poker sense: one hand, one trade, or one month is never enough to validate a method. What matters is whether the process would survive repetition across many draws. He also highlights old speculative history, especially pre-regulatory markets, as a way to understand crypto because many crypto pathologies are just older market behaviors returning in rawer form. "Painting the tape" becomes the bridge example: a stock-operator trick from old ticker markets that reappears in thin crypto assets as wash-trade-driven attention farming.

The second half of the discussion shifts from process to structure. The speakers argue that crypto prices are deeply narrative-driven, especially where valuation is uncertain. In that setting, price and narrative become reflexive: narratives lift prices, higher prices harden the narratives, and broken narratives can collapse price faster than fundamentals alone would suggest. This is their main explanation for why Bitcoin and Ethereum should not be evaluated the same way. Bitcoin, in their framing, benefits from a cleaner and more legible monetary story. Ethereum and other smart-contract systems may enable useful technology, but the value-accrual story is more complex, more fragmented, and therefore harder to coordinate around.

That leads to the strongest macro claim in the interview: Bitcoin is treated as crypto's primary [[concepts/shelling-point|shelling point]]. The idea is that many different people can arrive at Bitcoin for different reasons, but they still converge on the same asset. By contrast, altcoin ecosystems fragment attention, narrative, and capital across many competing tokens and sub-theses. Light and Su Zhu link this to [[concepts/trust-minimization|trust minimization]], fair launch, and the social difficulty of reproducing a monetary coordination point once one already exists.

The source is most worth returning to not for whether every 2020 market view aged well, but for the clarity of its trader's worldview: process over ego, invalidation over hope, asymmetry over excitement, and shelling-point coordination over technologically richer but narratively weaker alternatives.
