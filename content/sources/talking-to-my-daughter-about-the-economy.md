Yanis Varoufakis wrote this 2018 book as a direct, story-driven explanation of how capitalism works and why it produces recurring crises, aimed at his teenage daughter (and by extension any curious young reader). It grew out of a Greek publisher’s request during the 2013 debt crisis and deliberately avoids economist jargon in favor of myths, historical anecdotes, and thought experiments.

The book opens with the puzzle of extreme [[Inequality]] and asks why some societies accumulate vast wealth while others remain trapped in poverty. Varoufakis uses the historical example of Aboriginal Australia versus colonial England to show that inequality is not natural but the result of specific social and economic arrangements that emerged with [[Market Society]].

He then traces the birth of [[Market Society]], showing how the separation of people from their means of subsistence (land, tools, common resources) forced the majority into wage labor and created the conditions for [[Profit]]. The narrative moves to the deep connection between [[Debt and Profit]], using the Faust legend to illustrate how credit and the promise of future repayment became the engine of capitalist expansion.

[[Banking]] appears next as the mechanism that recycles money through the economy; when this [[Recycling (Economic)|recycling]] breaks down, [[Crises (Economic)|crises]] follow, as seen in the Great Depression and the Greek crisis. Varoufakis explains how banks create money through [[Money Creation|lending]] and why this power is both necessary and inherently unstable.

The middle chapters examine [[Labor Markets]] and the tendency toward [[Unemployment]] even when people are willing to work for very low wages, and the way machines and [[Automation]] haunt modern economies by displacing human labor while still requiring consumers who earn wages.

Later sections challenge the idea that money can ever be [[Apolitical Money|apolitical]], using the story of a Second World War POW camp to demonstrate how even the most controlled environments quickly develop markets, prices, and [[Economic Power|power relations]]. The final chapter confronts the fantasy that technology or markets can solve problems that are fundamentally [[Politics of Markets|political]].

In the epilogue Varoufakis offers a thought experiment about a perfect pleasure machine and argues that genuine human flourishing requires more than consumption—it requires the ability to shape the rules of the economy itself.

Worth noting: the book’s strength lies in its refusal to treat economics as a technical subject separate from history, power, and ethics. Every chapter returns to the same core claim: the economy is a human creation that can be understood and changed.
