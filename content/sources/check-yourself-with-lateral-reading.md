# Check Yourself with Lateral Reading Crash Course Navigating Digital Information 3

This Crash Course episode argues that web credibility cannot usually be judged by reading a page straight down. Many unreliable pages are designed to look authoritative when read vertically. The source's answer is [[concepts/lateral-reading|lateral reading]]: leave the page, open new tabs, and investigate the source through the wider web.

## The Core Move

The strongest idea in the episode is that online evaluation is source-first rather than page-first. Instead of asking whether a page looks polished, ask who made it, why they made it, and what other credible sources say about them. The "Stop City-Funded Internet" example is useful because it shows how a page can present itself as grassroots local concern while actually serving a commercial incumbent's interests.

## Why Vertical Reading Fails

The source is especially good at puncturing the aesthetic cues people over-trust: clean design, citations, professional photos, `.org` branding, and grammatically polished copy. These signals can be staged. A site can look calm and civic while hiding the real incentives behind it.

## Practical Web-Native Verification

The episode recommends leaving the page, searching the organization, checking Wikipedia as a starting point, using fact-checking sites, and comparing how multiple sources describe the same actor or claim. It treats this as a new reading style required by the web itself rather than as a special trick for experts.

Worth coming back to: the page's most durable contribution is not only the phrase "lateral reading." It is the idea that credibility online is often discovered somewhere other than where the claim first appears.
