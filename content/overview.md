## Unified Model of Learning

### 1. Working Memory Is the Bottleneck
Your conscious mind can hold only a few chunks at once. Everything else must live in long-term memory. Effective learning encodes knowledge into long-term memory as [[concepts/chunking|chunks]] that can be loaded into working memory on demand. See [[concepts/working-memory-vs-long-term-memory]].

### 2. Retrieval Practice Is King
Both learning sources identify recall as the key technique. Rereading, highlighting, and following along create [[concepts/illusions-of-competence|illusions of competence]]. The fix: close the book, retrieve, then check.

### 3. Spaced Repetition Locks It In
Memory fades unless retrieved at increasing intervals. [[concepts/spaced-repetition|Spaced repetition]] strengthens memory because it is effortful.

### 4. Prerequisites Are Non-Negotiable
Missing prerequisites do not just slow you down; they create compounding gaps. See [[concepts/prereq-mastery]].

### 5. Deliberate Practice at the Edge
Both sources emphasize [[concepts/deliberate-practice|deliberate practice]]: working on material just beyond your current ability, not the comfortable material you already know.

### 6. Two Modes of Thinking
Oakley's unique contribution is [[concepts/focused-vs-diffuse-thinking|focused vs diffuse thinking]]. Hard focused work is necessary, but diffuse-mode breaks help unexpected connections emerge.

## Practical Takeaways

1. Use retrieval practice, not rereading.
2. Space your practice.
3. Master prerequisites first.
4. Focus on the hard stuff.
5. Alternate focused work with breaks.
6. Use the Pomodoro technique.
7. Exercise and sleep; they materially support learning.
8. Train at high volume.
9. Build where building compounds.
10. In investing and markets, distinguish durable process from temporary outcome.
11. AI is accelerating faster than prior technology shifts; build judgment and specific knowledge that compounds even as execution gets automated.
12. The AGI timeline (~2027) means career and skill decisions made today play out against a backdrop of radical change -- prioritize adaptability and leverage.
13. Build habits as systems, not goals. Start with identity ("who do I want to become?"), use environment design and the Two-Minute Rule to reduce friction, and track consistency not outcomes.
14. Habits automate the basics; deliberate practice pushes the frontier. Both are necessary for mastery.
15. Build a latticework of mental models from all major disciplines. Single-discipline thinking (man-with-a-hammer) produces reliable errors; multidisciplinary fluency produces disproportionately good outcomes.
16. Use a checklist of psychological tendencies when making high-stakes decisions. When multiple tendencies converge (lollapalooza), expect extreme outcomes and design systems with anti-gaming features.
17. Treat every visible success as one path through randomness. Ask what alternative histories, missing failures, hidden skew, and induction limits would say before calling an outcome skill.
18. Treat agent instruction files as workflow infrastructure. Keep project facts, behavior rules, decision memory, failure memory, and verification checks in files the relevant agent actually reads.
19. Spend more time framing hard problems before solving them; alternative diagnoses, root causes, and stakeholder views often change the answer completely.
20. Track both delta and level: honor progress from origin, but calibrate strategy against the real field you want to enter.
21. Fallacies are not just textbook mistakes; they repeatedly drive bad medical, legal, political, and scientific judgment when fear, habit, or analogy substitute for evidence.
22. Strong reasoning often comes down to a few recurring checks: what is the causal mechanism, who bears the burden of proof, are the compared cases relevantly similar, is the feared chain really probable, is the midpoint actually just, and has the argument assumed what it is trying to prove.
23. Good critical thinking is both procedural and dialogic: use a simple decision loop for everyday choices, and use Socratic questioning when a belief needs to be stress-tested more deeply.
